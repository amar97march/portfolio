In March 2023, the Future of Life Institute published an open letter calling for a six-month pause on the training of AI systems more powerful than GPT-4. Thousands signed it, including prominent researchers and tech leaders. The letter argued that AI labs were locked in a reckless race to develop and deploy increasingly powerful systems with insufficient safety measures.

The letter ignited a debate that has only intensified since. Should we pause AI development? Regulate it heavily? Or is the current trajectory—rapid development with evolving guardrails—the best path forward?

I have spent over 250 days writing about AI, studying its capabilities, its risks, and the regulatory landscape. Here is my honest assessment.

---

### The Case for Pausing AI Development

Proponents of a pause make several compelling arguments:

**1. We Do Not Understand What We Are Building**
The honest truth is that we do not fully understand how large language models work. We know how to train them. We know they produce impressive outputs. But the internal mechanisms—why they generate specific responses, what they have "learned" versus memorized, whether they have emergent capabilities we have not discovered yet—remain partially opaque.

Building increasingly powerful systems that we do not fully understand is, the argument goes, reckless.

**2. Safety Research Is Falling Behind**
AI capabilities research receives far more funding, talent, and attention than AI safety research. The gap between what AI systems can do and what we can verify about their behavior is growing. A pause would allow safety research to catch up.

**3. Competitive Pressure Compromises Safety**
AI companies face intense competitive pressure. Being first to market with a more powerful model brings enormous commercial advantage. This creates incentives to cut corners on safety testing and rush deployment. A coordinated pause would remove this pressure.

**4. Democratic Deliberation Takes Time**
Society needs time to understand AI, debate its implications, and develop appropriate governance frameworks. AI is advancing faster than democratic institutions can respond. A pause would give society breathing room.

### The Case Against Pausing

Critics of a pause raise equally compelling counterarguments:

**1. A Pause Is Unenforceable**
Who would enforce a global AI development pause? There is no international body with the authority or capacity to monitor compliance. Countries or organizations that do not participate would gain a massive advantage, potentially concentrating AI power in the hands of those least likely to develop it responsibly.

**2. A Pause Freezes the Wrong Things**
A blanket pause would halt safety research alongside capabilities research. Many of the most promising approaches to AI safety—RLHF, constitutional AI, interpretability research—require building and experimenting with powerful models. You cannot develop safety for systems you are not allowed to build.

**3. AI Is Already Providing Benefits**
AI is being used in drug discovery, climate modeling, accessibility tools, medical diagnostics, and countless other beneficial applications. A pause would delay these benefits, and the human cost of that delay is real.

**4. The Definition Problem**
What exactly would be paused? Models above a certain size? Certain architectures? Certain applications? Any threshold is arbitrary, easily circumvented, and likely to become irrelevant as techniques improve. A compute threshold, for instance, becomes meaningless as training efficiency advances.

**5. It Creates a False Sense of Security**
A six-month pause might make people feel safer without actually making AI safer. The fundamental challenges—alignment, interpretability, robustness—are not going to be solved in six months. The pause could end with the same problems and the same competitive pressures.

---

### The Case for Regulation (Without Pausing)

The middle ground—and the path most governments have chosen—is regulation without a development moratorium:

**1. Targeted Regulation Works**
Rather than pausing all development, regulate specific applications based on risk. This is exactly what the EU AI Act does. High-risk applications face strict requirements. Low-risk applications face minimal oversight. This allows beneficial development to continue while protecting against specific harms.

**2. Standards and Accountability**
Regulation can require safety testing, documentation, transparency, and human oversight without halting development. The goal is not to stop AI but to ensure it is developed responsibly.

**3. Liability and Incentives**
Legal liability for AI harms creates financial incentives for safety that complement technical research. If companies know they will face consequences for deploying unsafe AI, they will invest more in safety—without needing a mandated pause.

**4. International Coordination**
While a global pause is unrealistic, international regulatory coordination is achievable. The OECD AI Principles, the G7 Hiroshima Process, and bilateral agreements between major AI-developing nations can create shared standards without requiring a blanket moratorium.

### The Arguments I Find Most Persuasive

After deep study, here is where I land:

**A blanket pause is impractical and counterproductive.** It is unenforceable, would not actually solve the underlying problems, and would sacrifice real benefits. The open letter, while well-intentioned, proposed a solution that does not match the complexity of the problem.

**However, the concerns that motivated the call for a pause are legitimate.** We are developing increasingly powerful AI systems without fully understanding them. Safety research is underfunded. Competitive pressure does compromise safety. These are real problems that demand real solutions.

**Smart regulation is the answer, but it must be more aggressive than what we have today.** Here is what I believe is needed:

**1. Mandatory Safety Evaluations**
Before deploying any model above a certain capability threshold, companies should be required to conduct and publish standardized safety evaluations. Not voluntary. Not self-reported. Independently verified.

**2. Significant Investment in Safety Research**
A percentage of AI development spending—I would suggest at least 20%—should be directed toward safety and alignment research. This could be enforced through regulation or incentivized through tax policy.

**3. Transparency Requirements**
AI companies should be required to disclose the broad parameters of their training processes, the safety tests conducted, and the known limitations of their systems. Trade secrets can be protected while still providing meaningful transparency.

**4. Incident Reporting**
There should be mandatory incident reporting for AI systems, similar to what exists in aviation and healthcare. When an AI system causes harm, there should be a structured process for investigating and learning from the incident.

**5. Graduated Deployment**
High-capability models should follow a graduated deployment process—limited beta, expanded beta, general availability—with safety assessments at each stage. Rushing from development to mass deployment should be the exception, not the norm.

**6. International Safety Standards**
The AI safety summits that began at Bletchley Park need to produce binding international standards, not just aspirational principles. This is the hardest piece politically, but it is essential.

---

### The Meta-Problem

There is a meta-problem that underpins this entire debate: **the people most qualified to assess AI risks are often the same people who profit from AI development.** This creates a structural conflict of interest that no amount of voluntary commitment can resolve.

Independent safety research, independent auditing, and independent regulatory bodies are essential. The fox cannot guard the henhouse, no matter how well-intentioned the fox may be.

### What Individual Developers Can Do

Regardless of what governments do, individual developers have agency:

1. **Take safety seriously in your own work.** Test for bias. Document your systems. Build in human oversight. Do not deploy systems you would not be comfortable explaining to a regulator.

2. **Support safety research.** If you have the skills, contribute to alignment and interpretability research. If not, advocate for safety investment within your organization.

3. **Engage with the policy process.** Comment on proposed regulations. Participate in standards development. Your technical expertise is valuable in shaping policy.

4. **Be honest about limitations.** Do not oversell your AI systems. Be transparent about what they can and cannot do. Hype culture is a safety risk.

5. **Push back on reckless timelines.** If your organization is rushing deployment without adequate safety testing, say something. It is not always easy, but it matters.

---

### My Bottom Line

The question should not be "pause or not?" The question should be: "What specific mechanisms will ensure that AI development proceeds in a way that is safe, beneficial, and accountable?"

A pause is a blunt instrument for a nuanced problem. What we need is a sharp scalpel: targeted regulation, mandatory safety standards, independent oversight, and a cultural shift in the AI industry from "move fast and break things" to "move thoughtfully and build things that work."

The technology is too important to develop recklessly. It is also too important to stop.
