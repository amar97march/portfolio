Over the past two weeks, we have done a deep dive into AI for Good — climate modeling, astronomy, protein folding, accessibility, agriculture, conservation, disaster response, education, and the digital divide. It has been intense, sometimes sobering, and consistently fascinating.

Today, I want to step back from the technical deep dives and share something more personal: a curated collection of AI for Good projects that genuinely inspire me. These are not necessarily the most famous or well-funded projects. They are the ones that make me feel that this technology is worth dedicating a career to.

---

### 1. The Ocean Cleanup's AI-Powered Trash Detection

**The Ocean Cleanup** is a nonprofit engineering organization working to remove plastic from the world's oceans. One of their key challenges is locating ocean plastic — the Great Pacific Garbage Patch is not a solid island of trash, but a diffuse zone where plastic is mixed with seawater across millions of square kilometers.

They use AI to analyze aerial imagery and satellite data to **map plastic concentrations**, directing their cleanup systems to the densest areas. Computer vision models distinguish plastic debris from natural materials (seaweed, seafoam, driftwood) in drone imagery — a surprisingly difficult classification problem.

What inspires me: the combination of physical engineering (the cleanup systems) with AI (the detection and targeting). Neither alone would be sufficient.

### 2. Crisis Text Line: AI for Mental Health Triage

**Crisis Text Line** is a free, 24/7 text-based mental health service. Millions of people, especially young people who are uncomfortable calling a phone line, text with trained crisis counselors when they are struggling.

The challenge: with limited counselors and many incoming messages, how do you ensure that the person in most acute danger is connected with help first? Their AI system analyzes incoming messages and **triages conversations by severity**, flagging messages that suggest imminent risk so counselors can prioritize.

The system does not replace human counselors — it helps them allocate their limited time where it matters most. Their analysis has revealed that certain phrases and patterns are strongly predictive of active crisis, insights that have improved training for counselors.

What inspires me: AI as a force multiplier for human compassion. The counselors provide the empathy and intervention. AI ensures that empathy reaches the people who need it most urgently.

### 3. Wadhwani AI: AI for Low-Resource Healthcare

**Wadhwani AI**, based in India, builds AI solutions specifically for challenges in developing countries. Their projects include:

- **AI-powered screening for cotton pest identification**, helping Indian farmers reduce crop losses
- **AI for newborn anthropometry** — using smartphone cameras to estimate the weight and length of newborns, enabling early identification of underweight babies in communities without scales
- **Tuberculosis screening** from chest X-rays, designed to work with the lower-quality imaging equipment common in rural clinics

What inspires me: the deliberate focus on problems that matter in low-resource settings, not problems that are easy to publish papers about. They build for the constraints (limited connectivity, basic devices, non-specialist users) rather than assuming ideal conditions.

### 4. Endangered Language Documentation with AI

Of the world's approximately 7,000 languages, **nearly half are endangered** — likely to disappear within a generation or two. When a language dies, an entire way of understanding the world is lost.

Several projects use AI to accelerate language documentation:

- **Automatic speech recognition** for endangered languages, trained on small amounts of audio data
- **Machine translation** between endangered languages and major languages, enabling community members to create bilingual educational materials
- **AI-assisted transcription** of oral histories and stories, converting audio recordings of elders into written text

The technical challenge is severe — these languages have minimal training data. Techniques like **transfer learning** (adapting models trained on well-resourced languages) and **few-shot learning** are essential.

What inspires me: this is AI used to preserve human diversity, not homogenize it. In a world where AI often accelerates the dominance of English, these projects push back.

### 5. Wildlife Insights: Democratizing Conservation AI

**Wildlife Insights** is a collaboration between Google, the Wildlife Conservation Society, WWF, and other organizations. It provides a cloud platform where conservation teams anywhere in the world can upload camera trap images and receive AI-powered species identification for free.

The platform has processed millions of images, automatically identifying hundreds of species. This turns camera trap data from a bottleneck (months of manual image review) into a rapid assessment tool.

What inspires me: the democratization aspect. Small conservation organizations in developing countries with no AI expertise can access the same analytical capabilities as well-funded research institutions.

### 6. Project CETI: Decoding Whale Communication

**Project CETI** (Cetacean Translation Initiative) is an ambitious effort to use machine learning to understand the communication system of sperm whales. Sperm whales communicate using clicks called **codas** — sequences of clicks with specific timing patterns.

The project uses underwater microphones, drones, and robotic tags to collect vast amounts of whale audio data, then applies NLP techniques (originally developed for human language) to analyze the structure and meaning of codas.

While "translating" whale language is a long-term goal, even partial understanding of their communication could transform marine conservation — helping us understand their social structures, migration decisions, and responses to human-caused disturbances.

What inspires me: the sheer ambition and the philosophical implications. If we can use AI to understand another species' communication system, it fundamentally changes our relationship with the natural world.

---

### 7. Open Climate Fix: Solar Energy Forecasting

**Open Climate Fix** is a nonprofit that uses AI to improve solar energy forecasting for the UK power grid. Better forecasts of solar panel output (which depends on cloud cover, which changes rapidly) reduce the need for fossil fuel backup power.

Their models use satellite imagery of cloud patterns combined with weather data to predict solar irradiance at specific locations minutes to hours in advance. Even small improvements in forecast accuracy translate to significant reductions in carbon emissions from the power grid.

What inspires me: the elegant simplicity. Better weather prediction for solar panels is not glamorous, but the carbon impact at grid scale is enormous. Not every AI for Good project needs to be moonshot-level ambitious.

---

### 8. AI2 Incubator: Semantic Scholar

The **Allen Institute for AI** (AI2), founded by the late Paul Allen, builds AI tools for scientific research. Their **Semantic Scholar** platform uses AI to make the entire corpus of scientific literature searchable by meaning, not just keywords. We covered this in detail on Day 215.

What inspires me: the long-term vision. AI2 was founded with the explicit mission of building AI for the common good, funded by philanthropy rather than advertising revenue. Their decision to make Semantic Scholar free and open reflects a belief that access to knowledge should not be gated by ability to pay.

### What These Projects Have in Common

Looking across these projects, several patterns emerge:

1. **They solve real problems for underserved populations.** Not hypothetical problems or problems for people who already have abundant resources.

2. **They augment human capability rather than replacing it.** AI triages for counselors, identifies species for conservationists, screens for doctors. The human remains central.

3. **They design for constraints.** Limited data, limited connectivity, limited technical expertise. The best AI for Good projects meet users where they are.

4. **They are often open-source or freely available.** Access is a feature, not an afterthought.

5. **They combine AI with domain expertise.** The AI researchers work alongside marine biologists, linguists, doctors, and farmers. The technology serves the domain, not the other way around.

### How to Get Involved

If these projects inspire you as they inspire me, here are ways to contribute:

- **Contribute to open-source AI for Good projects** on GitHub
- **Volunteer your AI skills** through organizations like DataKind, AI for Good Foundation, or Omdena
- **Work on underrepresented languages** through Masakhane or Common Voice
- **Build for accessibility** — even small improvements make a difference
- **Choose problems that matter** when deciding where to focus your career

### Final Thoughts

The AI industry spends most of its energy optimizing advertising, generating content, and building tools for knowledge workers in wealthy countries. There is nothing wrong with these applications, but they represent a tiny slice of what AI could do.

The projects I have highlighted today show what is possible when talented people point AI at the world's most pressing problems. They remind me why I got interested in this field in the first place.

Technology is not inherently good or bad. It is a lever. And the most important question is not how powerful the lever is, but what you choose to move with it.
