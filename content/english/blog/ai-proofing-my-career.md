I have spent the last several posts analyzing how AI will affect jobs, which roles are at risk, and what new opportunities are emerging. But abstract analysis is different from personal strategy. In this post, I want to get personal and share exactly what I am doing to keep my own career relevant in an AI-transformed world.

This is not a prescriptive guide. Every career is different. But I hope my thought process is useful to others navigating the same questions.

---

### My Starting Position

I am an AI/ML professional. You might think that makes me safe—after all, AI expertise is in high demand. But the truth is more nuanced:

- The specific tools and techniques I know today will be outdated within a few years.
- AI is increasingly automating the work of AI practitioners themselves. AutoML, AI-assisted coding, and no-code AI platforms are making it easier for non-specialists to build AI systems.
- The barrier to entry in AI/ML is dropping. More people are entering the field every year, increasing competition.

Being "in AI" does not make me immune to disruption by AI. It just means I have a front-row seat.

---

### Strategy 1: Move Up the Abstraction Ladder

AI is automating tasks from the bottom up. The most routine, well-defined tasks go first. My strategy is to continuously move toward higher-level work:

**From coding to architecting.** Instead of spending my time writing model training code (which AI assistants can do increasingly well), I focus on system design—how do you architect an AI system that is reliable, scalable, and maintainable?

**From building to evaluating.** Instead of just building models, I invest time in developing evaluation frameworks—how do you rigorously test whether an AI system is working correctly and safely?

**From execution to strategy.** Instead of just executing AI projects, I work on identifying which problems are worth solving with AI and which are not. The ability to say "we should not use AI here" is surprisingly valuable.

The principle: **do the work that requires judgment, context, and accountability—the work that AI cannot verify for itself.**

### Strategy 2: Build T-Shaped Expertise

The metaphor of "T-shaped" skills has never been more relevant. The vertical bar of the T represents deep expertise in a specific domain. The horizontal bar represents broad knowledge across many areas.

My approach:

**Deep expertise** in ML engineering, AI safety, and system design. I maintain this through continuous learning, building projects, and staying current with research.

**Broad knowledge** across adjacent domains: software engineering, product management, data governance, AI regulation, and business strategy. I maintain this through reading, conversations, and deliberate cross-functional work.

The deep expertise makes me valuable. The broad knowledge makes me versatile. The combination makes me difficult to replace—whether by another person or by an AI.

---

### Strategy 3: Invest in Uniquely Human Skills

The skills that AI cannot replicate are the skills that will hold their value longest:

**Communication.** The ability to explain complex technical concepts to non-technical stakeholders. The ability to write clearly. The ability to present persuasively. I invest in these skills through this blog, through presentations, and through deliberate practice.

**Relationship building.** Technical skills get you in the door. Relationships determine your trajectory. I invest in building genuine professional relationships—mentoring, collaborating, and engaging with the community.

**Judgment under uncertainty.** Most important decisions are made with incomplete information. The ability to make good judgment calls—when to push forward, when to pivot, when to wait—is irreplaceable. I develop this through experience and reflection.

**Teaching.** The ability to teach others is a multiplier on all other skills. It forces you to understand concepts deeply, builds your reputation, and creates a network of people who associate you with expertise. This blog is part of that strategy.

---

### Strategy 4: Learn to Work With AI, Not Against It

I use AI tools extensively in my own work:

- **AI coding assistants** for writing boilerplate code, debugging, and refactoring.
- **AI research tools** for literature review, summarization, and brainstorming.
- **AI writing tools** for drafting, editing, and improving my writing.

But I use them as tools, not replacements. I always review AI-generated code before committing it. I verify AI research outputs against primary sources. I edit and refine AI-generated drafts to reflect my voice and judgment.

The meta-skill is knowing **when to use AI and when not to.** Sometimes the "inefficiency" of doing something manually is the whole point—it builds understanding, catches edge cases, and produces work that is genuinely mine.

```python
# My personal framework for deciding when to use AI

def should_use_ai(task: dict) -> dict:
    """
    Decision framework for AI-assisted vs. manual work.
    """
    # Tasks where AI adds clear value
    ai_strong = [
        "boilerplate_code",
        "initial_drafts",
        "literature_search",
        "data_formatting",
        "brainstorming",
        "refactoring",
        "translation",
    ]

    # Tasks where manual work is preferable
    manual_strong = [
        "architectural_decisions",
        "stakeholder_communication",
        "ethical_judgments",
        "relationship_building",
        "creative_direction",
        "learning_new_concepts",  # The struggle is the point
        "code_review_final",  # Accountability requires understanding
    ]

    task_type = task.get("type", "")

    if task_type in ai_strong:
        return {
            "recommendation": "Use AI",
            "reason": "Well-defined task where AI saves significant time",
            "caveat": "Always review output before using",
        }
    elif task_type in manual_strong:
        return {
            "recommendation": "Do manually",
            "reason": "Task requires judgment, accountability, or "
                      "deep understanding",
            "caveat": "AI can assist but should not lead",
        }
    else:
        return {
            "recommendation": "Hybrid approach",
            "reason": "Use AI for initial pass, apply human "
                      "judgment for refinement",
            "caveat": "Evaluate AI contribution quality carefully",
        }
```

### Strategy 5: Build a Public Body of Work

This blog series is perhaps my most deliberate career strategy. By writing consistently about AI, I am:

1. **Building expertise.** Writing forces me to understand topics deeply. I cannot explain something well unless I truly understand it.

2. **Building visibility.** A public body of work is a form of professional insurance. Even if my current role changes, my writing demonstrates expertise and commitment.

3. **Building a network.** Writing attracts readers, conversations, and connections that would not happen otherwise.

4. **Creating an archive.** My thinking about AI is documented in hundreds of posts. This is a resource I can draw on, update, and build upon.

The principle: **in a world where AI can generate generic content instantly, original thinking and authentic voice become more valuable, not less.**

---

### Strategy 6: Stay Financially Resilient

Career transitions—whether voluntary or forced—require financial resilience. I maintain:

- An emergency fund covering several months of expenses.
- Investments in my own skills and education.
- Multiple income-related activities (not just a single employer).
- Low fixed costs relative to income.

This is not AI-specific advice. It is career advice that becomes more important in a rapidly changing environment.

---

### Strategy 7: Embrace Uncertainty

Perhaps the most important strategy is psychological: **accepting that I cannot predict the future of my career with certainty.** AI is evolving so rapidly that any specific five-year career plan will be outdated within two years.

Instead of planning for a specific future, I prepare for a range of futures:
- A future where AI augments my current work (most likely).
- A future where AI significantly changes the nature of my work (plausible).
- A future where AI disrupts my current role entirely (possible but unlikely in the near term).

The strategies above—moving up the abstraction ladder, building T-shaped expertise, investing in human skills, learning to work with AI, building a public body of work, and staying financially resilient—are robust across all these scenarios.

---

### What I Am Not Doing

It is equally important to share what I am not doing:

- **I am not panicking.** Fear-driven career decisions are rarely good ones.
- **I am not ignoring AI.** Denial is just as dangerous as panic.
- **I am not trying to predict the specific trajectory of AI.** Nobody can.
- **I am not abandoning technical skills.** Even as I invest in broader skills, deep technical capability remains my foundation.
- **I am not waiting for someone else to AI-proof my career.** No employer, government, or institution will do this for me.

---

### The Honest Truth

Here is the honest truth: I do not know exactly how AI will affect my career over the next decade. Nobody does. The field is moving too fast for anyone to predict with confidence.

What I do know is that the professionals who will fare best are those who are **actively engaged** with the technology—using it, understanding it, thinking critically about it, and continuously adapting their skills and strategies.

That is what I am trying to do. Every day. Including today, writing this post.
