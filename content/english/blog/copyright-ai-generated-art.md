When Jason Allen won first place in the digital art category at the 2022 Colorado State Fair with an image generated using Midjourney, the backlash was immediate. Artists felt that an AI-generated piece should not compete alongside human-created art. But buried beneath the emotional debate was a legal question that is far more consequential: who owns the copyright to AI-generated art? The person who typed the prompt? The company that built the AI? The artists whose work trained the model? Or no one at all?

This question is not academic. It affects every person who uses an AI tool to create images, music, text, or video. It affects every business that incorporates AI-generated content into its products, marketing, or operations. And it affects every artist whose livelihood depends on the legal protections that copyright provides. The legal landscape is evolving rapidly, with court decisions, regulatory guidance, and legislative proposals appearing at a pace that makes it difficult even for legal specialists to keep current.

This post provides a comprehensive examination of the copyright ownership question for AI-generated content: the legal foundations, the key cases, the international perspective, and the practical implications for creators and businesses.

## The Fundamental Question: Does AI-Generated Art Qualify for Copyright?

Before asking who owns the copyright to AI-generated art, we must first ask whether AI-generated art can be copyrighted at all. The answer, under current law in most jurisdictions, is more complex than it might initially appear.

### The Human Authorship Requirement

Copyright law in the United States, and in most countries that follow the Berne Convention, requires that a work be created by a human author to qualify for copyright protection. This requirement is not explicitly stated in the Copyright Act itself, but it has been consistently interpreted by the US Copyright Office and the courts.

The foundational case is Burrow-Giles Lithographic Co. v. Sarony (1884), in which the Supreme Court held that a photograph qualified for copyright protection because the photographer made creative choices about posing, lighting, and composition. The Court defined "author" as the person to whom the work owes its origin, the person who creates it through intellectual conception.

More recently, in Naruto v. Slater (2018), the Ninth Circuit held that a monkey that took selfies could not own copyright because copyright is limited to human creators. While the case involved an animal rather than an AI, the principle is the same: only humans can be authors under US copyright law.

### The US Copyright Office's Position

The US Copyright Office has taken a clear and increasingly detailed position on AI-generated works. In February 2023, the Office issued a letter regarding the registration of Zarya of the Dawn, a graphic novel created by Kris Kashtanova using Midjourney-generated images. The Office granted copyright registration for the text and the selection and arrangement of the images, but denied registration for the individual AI-generated images themselves.

The Office's reasoning was that Midjourney's image generation process involves sufficient unpredictability and randomness that the user cannot be said to be the author of the specific images produced. Even though the user provides a prompt and may iterate through many variations, the specific visual expression is determined by the AI model, not the human.

In March 2023, the Copyright Office issued further guidance stating that AI-generated content without human authorship cannot be registered. However, works that combine AI-generated elements with sufficient human creative expression may be registrable to the extent of the human contributions.

This creates a spectrum:

At one end, a purely AI-generated image, created by typing a simple prompt into Midjourney or DALL-E, receives no copyright protection.

In the middle, a work that combines AI-generated elements with significant human creative modification, selection, arrangement, or enhancement may receive copyright protection for the human-authored portions.

At the other end, a work where a human uses AI as a tool but exercises sufficient creative control over the output, comparable to using Photoshop or a camera, may be fully copyrightable if the human's creative choices are reflected in the final work.

### The Thaler v. Perlmutter Decision

The question was tested directly in Thaler v. Perlmutter (2023), where Stephen Thaler sought to register a copyright for an image generated entirely by his AI system, DABUS, with DABUS listed as the author. The US District Court for the District of Columbia ruled against Thaler, holding that copyright requires human authorship and that an AI system cannot be an author.

The court acknowledged that the human authorship requirement might need to evolve as AI technology develops but concluded that this evolution must come from Congress, not the courts. The decision reinforced the Copyright Office's position and established the first federal court ruling directly addressing AI authorship.

## The Prompt Engineering Question

One of the most interesting sub-debates concerns whether writing a detailed, creative prompt constitutes sufficient authorship to qualify the resulting image for copyright protection.

### The Argument for Prompt Authorship

Proponents argue that a skilled prompt engineer exercises significant creative judgment: choosing subject matter, specifying style, selecting composition, defining lighting, and iterating through numerous variations to achieve a desired result. This creative process, they argue, is analogous to a photographer directing a scene or a film director guiding actors. The human provides the creative vision, and the AI (like a camera or a team of technicians) executes it.

A detailed prompt like "an oil painting in the style of the Hudson River School depicting a misty autumn morning in the Catskill Mountains with a lone figure on a stone bridge, warm golden light filtering through ancient oaks, with particular attention to atmospheric perspective and the interplay of light and shadow on water" reflects substantial creative judgment. The argument is that this creative judgment should be recognized as authorship.

### The Argument Against Prompt Authorship

Opponents argue that a prompt, no matter how detailed, does not give the user sufficient control over the specific expression of the resulting image. The same prompt entered into the same model will produce a different image each time. The user cannot predict or control the specific arrangement of pixels, the exact brushstrokes, or the precise composition. Because copyright protects specific expression rather than ideas, and the specific expression is determined by the AI model rather than the human, the prompt author is not the author of the image.

The Copyright Office has sided with the latter position, distinguishing between idea (which is what the prompt provides) and expression (which is what the model generates). Under traditional copyright doctrine, only expression is protectable, and the expression of an AI-generated image originates from the model, not the prompt.

### The Middle Ground: Iterative Processes

The most interesting cases involve iterative processes where the human provides a prompt, evaluates the result, modifies the prompt, generates new variations, selects the best result, and then further modifies it using editing tools. Each step involves human creative judgment, and the cumulative effect may rise to the level of authorship even if no single step does.

The Copyright Office's guidance suggests that this kind of iterative, hands-on process may support a claim of authorship, particularly when the human exercises meaningful control over the final output and the AI serves as a tool rather than the primary creative agent. But the line between "using AI as a tool" and "prompting AI to create" remains poorly defined and will likely require further litigation to clarify.

## International Approaches

The human authorship requirement and the treatment of AI-generated works vary significantly across jurisdictions.

### The United Kingdom

The UK is unusual in having explicit statutory provision for computer-generated works. Section 9(3) of the Copyright, Designs and Patents Act 1988 states that for a literary, dramatic, musical, or artistic work that is computer-generated, the author is the person by whom the arrangements necessary for the creation of the work are undertaken.

This provision was enacted long before modern AI and was originally intended to address relatively simple computer programs that generated output based on human-defined parameters. Its application to modern generative AI is debated. Under one interpretation, the person who prompts an AI model "makes the arrangements necessary" and is therefore the author. Under another interpretation, the AI company that designed and trained the model made the truly necessary arrangements, and the user merely triggered a pre-existing creative capability.

No UK court has yet definitively applied Section 9(3) to modern AI-generated works, so the interpretation remains uncertain.

### The European Union

The EU has not adopted a specific provision for computer-generated works. EU copyright law requires originality, defined as the author's own intellectual creation, which presupposes a human author. The Court of Justice of the European Union (CJEU) has emphasized the importance of the author's creative choices and personal stamp in its originality jurisprudence.

Under this framework, purely AI-generated works without sufficient human creative input are unlikely to qualify for copyright protection in the EU. However, works where a human exercises significant creative control over the AI-generated output may qualify if the human's creative choices are reflected in the final work.

### China

Chinese courts have been more willing to extend copyright-like protection to AI-generated works. In a notable 2023 case, a Beijing court held that an AI-generated image could receive copyright protection, reasoning that the user's creative choices in prompting and selecting the image constituted sufficient authorship. This decision diverged from the US approach and was seen as reflecting China's pragmatic approach to AI governance, which tends to favor rapid AI adoption.

However, other Chinese courts have reached different conclusions, and the law in China remains unsettled. The Supreme People's Court has not yet issued definitive guidance on AI authorship.

### Japan

Japan's approach is complex. While Japanese copyright law requires human authorship, the cultural and legal environment is relatively permissive toward AI-generated content. Japan's AI strategy has emphasized enabling AI development, and the government has signaled openness to accommodating AI-generated works within the copyright framework.

## Practical Implications for Different Stakeholders

### For Individual Artists Using AI Tools

If you use AI tools to create art, the copyright status of your work depends on how much human creative input you contribute. Purely prompted images with minimal human modification are unlikely to be copyrightable in the US or EU. However, if you use AI-generated images as a starting point and then substantially modify, arrange, and enhance them using your own creative judgment, the resulting work may be copyrightable to the extent of your contributions.

Practical recommendations include documenting your creative process to demonstrate human authorship, treating purely AI-generated elements as uncopyrightable and not relying on copyright to protect them, using AI as one tool in a broader creative workflow rather than as the sole creator, and being transparent about AI's role in your creative process.

### For Businesses Using AI-Generated Content

Businesses that incorporate AI-generated content face significant risks if they assume that content is copyrightable. Marketing materials, product images, website content, and other AI-generated assets may not receive copyright protection, meaning competitors can freely copy them.

Practical recommendations include not relying on copyright protection for purely AI-generated content, considering AI-generated content as part of a larger work that includes sufficient human creative expression, ensuring that business-critical content includes meaningful human authorship, reviewing the terms of service of AI tools to understand rights and restrictions, and considering alternative intellectual property protections such as trade dress, trademarks, and contractual restrictions where copyright is unavailable.

### For AI Companies

AI companies face the challenge of clearly communicating the copyright status of their tools' outputs to users. Many users assume that because they paid for the tool and typed the prompt, they own the copyright. This assumption may be incorrect, and AI companies have a responsibility to educate their users.

Some AI companies have taken steps to address this. OpenAI's terms of service assign all rights in the output to the user (to the extent the output is copyrightable). Midjourney grants users ownership of their generated images. Adobe's Firefly was trained on licensed and public domain content specifically to reduce copyright risks for users. But these contractual provisions cannot create copyright where the law does not provide it. A contract can assign existing rights, but it cannot create a copyright in a work that is not copyrightable.

## The Broader Implications

### The Public Domain Flood

If AI-generated content is largely uncopyrightable, a vast quantity of creative content will enter the public domain by default. This has profound implications for the creative ecosystem. On one hand, it means a wealth of freely available visual, textual, and musical content that anyone can use. On the other hand, it means that creators who use AI tools may find it difficult to protect and monetize their work, potentially undermining the economic incentives for creative production.

### The Competitive Dynamics

The lack of copyright protection for AI-generated content creates interesting competitive dynamics. In industries where content differentiation is important, businesses cannot rely on copyright to prevent competitors from copying AI-generated content. This may shift competitive advantage toward factors other than content itself: brand, distribution, curation, personalization, and customer relationships.

### The Authentication and Provenance Challenge

If AI-generated content is not copyrightable but human-created content is, there is a strong incentive for creators to prove that their work is human-authored. This is driving interest in content provenance technologies, such as the Coalition for Content Provenance and Authenticity (C2PA) standard, which embeds cryptographic metadata in content to document its creation process.

However, provenance technologies are not foolproof. A creator can generate an image with AI, claim it as human-created, and potentially obtain copyright registration for it. The Copyright Office does not currently have the capacity to detect AI-generated content in registration applications, though it has begun to ask applicants to disclose AI involvement.

### The Chilling Effect on AI Art

Some artists worry that the lack of copyright protection for AI-generated art will create a chilling effect, discouraging the use of AI as a creative tool. If you cannot protect the output of your creative process, the argument goes, there is less incentive to invest time and effort in developing AI-assisted creative techniques.

Others argue the opposite: that the lack of copyright actually liberates AI art by removing the gatekeeping function of intellectual property and enabling a more open, collaborative creative culture.

## The Legislative Horizon

Several legislative proposals and policy discussions are underway that could change the copyright framework for AI-generated works.

### Proposals for a Sui Generis Right

Some scholars and industry groups have proposed creating a new, sui generis (unique) intellectual property right specifically for AI-generated works. This right would provide a shorter term of protection than traditional copyright (perhaps 5 to 15 years instead of life plus 70 years), different ownership rules (possibly vesting in the user, the AI company, or some combination), and different scope of protection (perhaps limited to exact or near-exact reproduction rather than the broader scope of copyright).

A sui generis right would acknowledge that AI-generated works are different from human-authored works while still providing some legal protection and economic incentive for their creation.

### Registration and Disclosure Requirements

Several proposals would require creators to disclose AI involvement when registering copyrights or publishing content. The US Copyright Office has already moved in this direction by requiring disclosure of AI-generated content in registration applications. More stringent disclosure requirements could help courts, consumers, and competitors distinguish between human-authored and AI-generated works.

### Compulsory Licensing for Training Data

As discussed in the context of the fair use debate, compulsory licensing schemes for AI training data could indirectly affect the copyright status of AI-generated works. If AI companies are required to compensate training data creators, the argument for extending copyright protection to AI outputs may become stronger, because the entire value chain would involve compensated creative contributions.

## The Philosophical Underpinning

At the deepest level, the copyright debate over AI-generated art forces us to confront fundamental questions about creativity, authorship, and value.

Traditional copyright theory justifies intellectual property protection on two grounds: the natural rights theory, which holds that creators have a moral right to the fruits of their creative labor, and the utilitarian theory, which holds that copyright incentivizes the creation of new works that benefit society.

For AI-generated art, neither theory provides a clean answer. The natural rights theory depends on identifying a human creator who invested creative labor, which is difficult when the AI model is the proximate cause of the specific expression. The utilitarian theory depends on whether copyright is necessary to incentivize AI-generated content. Given that AI can generate vast quantities of content at near-zero marginal cost, it is unclear that copyright incentives are needed.

This philosophical uncertainty is reflected in the legal uncertainty. Courts and legislatures are grappling with questions that existing intellectual property frameworks were not designed to answer. The resolution will require not just legal analysis but a broader societal conversation about what we value in creative works, who deserves to be rewarded for creating them, and how intellectual property law should adapt to a world where the line between human and machine creativity is increasingly blurred.

The only certainty in this space is that the law will continue to evolve. For creators, businesses, and technologists, staying informed about these developments is not optional. The decisions being made today about AI and copyright will shape the creative economy for generations to come, and understanding the legal landscape is essential for navigating it successfully.
