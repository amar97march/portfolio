The AI safety community is divided by a fundamental question: **Should we focus on the concrete harms AI is causing right now, or on the speculative but potentially catastrophic risks of superintelligent AI in the future?**

On one side, researchers and activists point to real, documented harms: biased hiring algorithms that discriminate against minorities, facial recognition systems used for mass surveillance, deepfakes undermining trust in information, and autonomous weapons that lower the threshold for violence. These harms are happening now, affecting real people, and demand immediate attention.

On the other side, researchers and thinkers argue that while current harms are real, they are manageable compared to the potential for an unaligned superintelligent AI to cause irreversible, civilization-ending damage. A few percentage points of bias in a hiring algorithm is a problem; a misaligned superintelligence is an existential catastrophe.

After a week of writing about AI safety, here is my honest attempt to navigate this debate.

---

### The Case for Prioritizing Current Risks

**1. Current harms are documented and real.**
AI-driven harms are not hypothetical. Algorithmic bias in criminal justice, credit scoring, and hiring has been extensively documented. Deepfakes have been used for fraud, harassment, and political manipulation. AI-powered surveillance is being used by authoritarian regimes to suppress dissent. These are measurable problems with identifiable victims.

**2. Current harms disproportionately affect marginalized communities.**
The people most harmed by biased AI are often those with the least power to push back: minorities, the poor, immigrants, and citizens of authoritarian states. Focusing exclusively on future existential risks—which tend to occupy wealthy technologists in affluent countries—ignores the people bearing the costs of AI today.

**3. Addressing current harms builds the infrastructure for future safety.**
The institutions, regulations, norms, and technical tools we develop to address current AI harms will be essential for addressing future risks. Fairness testing frameworks developed for hiring algorithms can be adapted for more powerful systems. Regulatory bodies trained on current AI will be better equipped to handle future AI. The path to long-term safety runs through short-term accountability.

**4. The speculative nature of existential risk makes it hard to act on.**
We do not know when or if AGI will arrive. We do not know what form it will take. We do not know which safety approaches will work. Meanwhile, current harms have clear causes, clear solutions, and clear urgency. Prioritizing speculative risks over concrete harms seems backwards.

### The Case for Prioritizing Existential Risk

**1. The magnitude is incomparable.**
Algorithmic bias harms thousands or millions. An unaligned superintelligence could end human civilization. By any expected-value calculation, even a small probability of extinction outweighs large-scale but bounded harms.

**2. Current harms are reversible; extinction is not.**
If a biased algorithm harms people, the algorithm can be fixed, reparations can be made, and policies can be changed. If a superintelligent AI causes civilizational collapse, there is no redo. The irreversibility of existential risk justifies disproportionate attention.

**3. The window for action may be closing.**
If AGI is achievable within decades—and many researchers believe it is—then the time to develop alignment techniques and governance frameworks is now, not after AGI exists. Retroactive safety for a superintelligent system is likely impossible.

**4. Resources are not actually zero-sum.**
The people working on existential risk are generally not the same people who would be working on algorithmic fairness. They have different skill sets, different institutional homes, and different funding sources. Framing it as a trade-off is a false choice.

### The Misuse Spectrum

To bridge these perspectives, it helps to see AI risk as a spectrum rather than a binary:

**Tier 1: Individual Harm (Today)**
- Biased hiring algorithms rejecting qualified candidates
- Facial recognition misidentifying individuals
- AI-generated harassment and deepfake abuse
- Predatory targeting in advertising and finance

**Tier 2: Systemic Harm (Near-Term)**
- Mass labor displacement without adequate transition support
- Weaponization of AI-generated misinformation at scale
- Autonomous weapons lowering barriers to conflict
- Concentration of AI power in a few corporations or states

**Tier 3: Catastrophic Harm (Medium-Term)**
- AI-enabled bioweapons development
- Cascading failures in AI-managed critical infrastructure
- AI-powered authoritarian control systems
- Manipulation of democratic processes at scale

**Tier 4: Existential Harm (Long-Term)**
- Misaligned AGI pursuing goals incompatible with human survival
- Intelligence explosion leading to uncontrollable superintelligence
- Permanent power lock-in by a small group using advanced AI

The important insight is that **these tiers are connected**. The organizations, norms, and technical approaches we develop for Tier 1 problems form the foundation for addressing Tier 2, 3, and 4 problems. Ignoring current harms does not just fail current victims—it undermines our capacity to address future risks.

### Where I Land

**The biggest risk right now is AI misuse and the concentration of AI power.** We are already seeing AI used for surveillance, manipulation, and discrimination at scale. These harms are real, growing, and insufficiently addressed. Any serious AI safety agenda must prioritize them.

**But existential risk deserves serious attention and investment.** The logical arguments for its possibility are sound, and the stakes are too high to dismiss. Investing in alignment research, interpretability, and governance frameworks for advanced AI is prudent—not because we know AGI is imminent, but because the cost of being unprepared is unacceptable.

**The false choice between current and future risks is the real danger.** When the debate becomes "which risks matter more," it often serves the interests of those who want to address neither. AI companies that invoke existential risk to avoid accountability for current harms are just as misguided as activists who dismiss long-term safety research as a distraction.

**Both require the same fundamental shift: treating AI safety as essential rather than optional.** Whether the harm is algorithmic bias in a hiring tool or a misaligned AGI, the root cause is the same: prioritizing what AI can do over ensuring it does what we actually want.

---

### Practical Recommendations

For the broader AI ecosystem:
1. Fund and support both near-term and long-term safety research.
2. Regulate current AI applications while building governance frameworks for future ones.
3. Ensure diverse voices—including those of affected communities—shape AI safety priorities.
4. Resist the urge to dismiss either category of risk as unimportant.

For individual developers:
1. Address the harms you can see and measure in the systems you build today.
2. Build with alignment principles in mind, even for narrow AI applications.
3. Stay informed about both current harms and long-term risks.
4. Advocate for safety investment within your organization.

The question is not "which risk is bigger?" The question is "how do we build a safety ecosystem that addresses both?" The answer starts with recognizing that current harms and future risks are not competing priorities. They are two faces of the same fundamental challenge: ensuring that AI serves humanity rather than undermining it.
